Fever in a baby is one of the few parenting situations where the correct
response depends almost entirely on age, and where the usual "wait and see"
instinct can be wrong.

<Callout type="warning">

  **If your baby is under three months old and has a rectal temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or above,
  contact a doctor immediately — at any hour, even if your baby seems well.** This is not a
  wait-until-morning situation. Young infants can have serious infections with very few outward
  signs.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Fever = **38°C / 100.4°F rectally**.
- **Under 3 months:** any fever is urgent, regardless of appearance.
- **3–6 months:** call the same day.
- **Over 6 months:** how your baby looks and behaves matters more than the number.
- Fever itself is a normal immune response, not the illness.

</KeyTakeaways>

## How to take the temperature

Rectal is the reference standard in infants, and it is what a doctor will ask
for. Digital thermometers designed for the purpose, with a small amount of
lubricant, inserted no more than about 1.5cm.

Forehead and ear thermometers are convenient and are fine for tracking a trend
in an older baby, but they are less reliable and can miss a fever. Do not use an
axillary or forehead reading to reassure yourself about a newborn.

Never use a mercury thermometer.

## Thresholds by age

**Under 3 months — 38°C or above.** Contact a doctor now. Expect that your baby
will be assessed in person and may need tests. This threshold exists because
young infants have immature immune systems and can deteriorate quickly, and
because they show remarkably few signs until they are seriously unwell.

**3 to 6 months — 38°C or above.** Call your doctor the same day.

**Over 6 months.** The number matters less. A cheerful baby with a temperature
of 39°C who is drinking and interacting is usually less concerning than a
listless baby at 38.2°C. Call if the fever lasts more than about two days, or at
any point if you are worried.

## Red flags at any age

Seek urgent care regardless of the reading if your baby has:

- Difficulty breathing, grunting, or ribs pulling in with each breath
- A rash that does not fade when pressed with a glass
- A stiff neck, or a bulging soft spot
- Unusual sleepiness, or is very difficult to rouse
- A seizure
- Persistent vomiting, or signs of dehydration — no wet nappy for eight hours,
  no tears, dry mouth, sunken soft spot
- A weak, high-pitched or continuous cry
- Mottled, pale, blue or grey skin

## What fever is actually doing

Fever is the immune system working, not the disease. Bringing the number down
does not treat the infection and does not shorten it. The purpose of
paracetamol or ibuprofen is comfort — a baby who feels better will drink more,
and hydration is what actually matters.

Dose by **weight**, not age, and follow the packaging. Ibuprofen is generally
not given under three months or to a dehydrated baby. Never give aspirin to a
child.

Skip the cold baths and the alcohol rubs. They cause shivering, which raises
core temperature, and they are miserable.

<Callout type="doctor">

  Trust the pattern over the number. Parents are usually right when they say their baby "isn't
  themselves", and that observation is clinically useful. Say it plainly when you call.

</Callout>

## The thing nobody tells you

You will call about a fever that turns out to be a virus. That is the system
working correctly. Nobody at the other end of the phone thinks you wasted their
time, and the alternative — a parent who talks themselves out of calling about a
six-week-old — is the outcome these thresholds exist to prevent.
