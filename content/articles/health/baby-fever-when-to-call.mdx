With fever, your baby's age matters more than the reading on the thermometer, and
this is one of the few situations where the usual wait-and-see instinct can hurt
them.

<Callout type="warning">

  **If your baby is under three months old and has a rectal temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or above,
  contact a doctor immediately, at any hour, even if your baby seems well.** Do not wait until
  morning. Young infants carry serious infections with very few outward signs.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Fever = **38°C / 100.4°F rectally**.
- **Under 3 months:** any fever is urgent, regardless of appearance.
- **3–6 months:** call the same day.
- **Over 6 months:** how your baby looks and behaves matters more than the number.
- Fever is the immune system working. The infection is the illness.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Taking the temperature

Rectal is the reference standard in infants, and your doctor will ask for it. Use a
digital thermometer designed for the purpose, with a small amount of lubricant,
inserted no more than about 1.5cm.

Forehead and ear thermometers are convenient, and they work well enough for
tracking a trend in an older baby. They are also less reliable and can miss a
fever, so never let an axillary or forehead reading reassure you about a newborn.

Never use a mercury thermometer.

## Thresholds by age

**Under 3 months, 38°C or above.** Contact a doctor now. Expect that someone will
assess your baby in person and may run tests. Young infants have immature immune
systems, can deteriorate fast, and show few signs until they are seriously unwell,
which is why the threshold sits so low.

**3 to 6 months, 38°C or above.** Call your doctor the same day.

**Over 6 months.** The number matters less. A cheerful baby at 39°C who is drinking
and interacting is usually less concerning than a listless baby at 38.2°C. Call if
the fever lasts more than about two days, or at any point if you are worried.

## Red flags at any age

Seek urgent care regardless of the reading if your baby has:

- Difficulty breathing, grunting, or ribs pulling in with each breath
- A rash that does not fade when pressed with a glass
- A stiff neck, or a bulging soft spot
- Unusual sleepiness, or is very difficult to rouse
- A seizure
- Persistent vomiting, or signs of dehydration: no wet nappy for eight hours, no
  tears, dry mouth, sunken soft spot
- A weak, high-pitched or continuous cry
- Mottled, pale, blue or grey skin

## Paracetamol buys comfort, nothing more

Your baby's immune system produces the fever. Bringing the number down treats
nothing and shortens nothing. Give paracetamol or ibuprofen for comfort instead: a
baby who feels better drinks more, and hydration is the thing that protects them.

Dose by **weight**, not age, and follow the packaging. Avoid ibuprofen under three
months and in a dehydrated baby. Never give aspirin to a child.

Skip the cold baths and the alcohol rubs. They cause shivering, which raises core
temperature, and they are miserable.

## You will call about a virus

At some point you will call about a fever that turns out to be an ordinary virus,
which is the system working as designed. The clinician on the other end of the
phone will not think you wasted their time, and these thresholds exist to stop a
parent talking themselves out of calling about a six-week-old.
