Starting solids attracts more confidently-stated, mutually contradictory advice
than almost any other parenting topic. Rice cereal first. Never rice cereal.
Vegetables before fruit or they will reject vegetables forever. Delay allergens.
Definitely do not delay allergens.

Most of that is tradition. Here is what current guidance actually supports.

<KeyTakeaways>
  - Start at **around six months**, guided by readiness signs rather than a date. - The **order** of
  first foods barely matters. Iron-rich foods and variety matter. - Introduce **common allergens
  early** and keep them in the diet — delaying is no longer recommended. - Milk stays the main
  source of nutrition through the first year. Early solids are practice.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Readiness, not the calendar

Four signs, and you want all of them:

- Sits upright with support and holds their head steady
- Has lost the tongue-thrust reflex that pushes food back out
- Shows genuine interest in what you are eating
- Can bring objects to their mouth with some accuracy

A baby who watches your fork intently but cannot hold their head steady is
interested, not ready. Wait.

## What to offer

The traditional progression — single-grain cereal, then yellow vegetables, then
green, then fruit — is a convention, not a finding. There is no good evidence
that offering fruit early creates a sweet-preferring toddler; babies are born
preferring sweet.

What does matter:

**Iron.** Iron stores from birth start running down around six months, and
breastmilk is low in iron. Iron-rich foods should feature early and often:
well-cooked meat, lentils, beans, tofu, iron-fortified cereals. Pair with
vitamin-C-rich foods to help absorption.

**Variety and texture.** Exposure to a wide range of flavours and textures in
the first year is associated with better acceptance later. Repetition helps too
— a rejected food often needs eight or more exposures.

**Energy density.** Thin purées are mostly water. Once your baby is managing
food, thicken it.

<Callout type="warning">
  Choking hazards for under-fours: whole grapes and cherry tomatoes (quarter them lengthways), whole
  nuts, popcorn, raw hard vegetable chunks, and large blobs of nut butter. Honey should be avoided
  entirely before twelve months because of infant botulism risk.
</Callout>

## Allergens: early, and then regularly

This is the area where guidance genuinely changed, and where old advice is still
widely repeated.

Current recommendations favour introducing common allergens — peanut, egg, dairy,
wheat, soy, sesame, fish, shellfish, tree nuts — from around six months, and
then **keeping them in the diet regularly**. Introducing once and stopping does
not maintain tolerance.

Practical approach: one new allergen at a time, in a small amount, earlier in
the day, when your baby is well and you can watch them. Peanut as thinned smooth
butter, never whole or in large sticky blobs. Egg well cooked.

**Talk to your doctor before starting** if your baby has severe eczema, an
existing food allergy, or a strong family history — some infants are advised to
have allergen introduction supervised.

## Purées or baby-led weaning

Both work. The evidence does not clearly favour either for healthy term infants,
and most families end up doing a mix — spoon-feeding some things, handing over
others.

Choose based on what you can sustain. A parent who finds baby-led weaning
stressful will do it badly; a parent who finds purée-making tedious will stop.

## Milk is still the main event

Through the first year, breastmilk or formula remains the primary source of
nutrition. Solids in the early months are about learning to eat: managing
texture, coordinating a swallow, discovering that food is a thing.

If solids are displacing milk feeds significantly before nine months, or your
baby is losing interest in milk, raise it at your next check-up.
