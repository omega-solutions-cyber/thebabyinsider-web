Start solids and everyone tells you something different, with total confidence.
Rice cereal first, or never rice cereal. Vegetables before fruit or your baby
rejects vegetables for life. Delay the allergens, or do not delay them. Most of
that is tradition rather than evidence.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Start at **around six months**, guided by readiness signs rather than a date.
- The **order** of first foods barely matters. Iron-rich foods and variety matter.
- Introduce **common allergens early** and keep them in the diet. Guidance no longer supports delaying.
- Milk stays the main source of nutrition through the first year. Early solids are practice.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Four readiness signs

Wait for all four:

- Sits upright with support and holds their head steady
- Has lost the tongue-thrust reflex that pushes food back out
- Shows real interest in what you are eating
- Can bring objects to their mouth with some accuracy

A baby who watches your fork but cannot hold their head steady is interested. Wait
for the head control.

## First foods

The traditional progression, single-grain cereal then yellow vegetables then green
then fruit, is a convention. No study produced it, and nobody has shown that early
fruit creates a sweet-preferring toddler. Babies are born preferring sweet.

**Iron.** Your baby's iron stores start running down at around six months, and
breastmilk holds little iron. Offer iron-rich foods early and often: well-cooked
meat, lentils, beans, tofu, iron-fortified cereals. Serve them alongside
vitamin-C-rich foods to help absorption.

**Variety and texture.** Babies who meet a wide range of flavours and textures in
the first year accept more food later. Repetition helps too, and a rejected food
often needs eight or more tries. Watch the water content while you do it, because
thin purées carry very little energy; once your baby is managing food, thicken it.

<Callout type="warning">

  Choking hazards for under-fours: whole grapes and cherry tomatoes (quarter them lengthways), whole
  nuts, popcorn, raw hard vegetable chunks, and large blobs of nut butter. Avoid honey before twelve
  months because of infant botulism risk.

</Callout>

## Allergens: early, and then regularly

Allergy specialists reversed this advice years ago, and parents still repeat the
old version.

Current recommendations favour introducing common allergens (peanut, egg, dairy,
wheat, soy, sesame, fish, shellfish, tree nuts) from around six months, then
**keeping them in the diet regularly**. Introducing once and stopping does not
maintain tolerance.

In practice: one new allergen at a time, in a small amount, earlier in the day,
while your baby is well and you can watch them. Thin peanut butter until it is
smooth, and never give it whole or in large sticky blobs. Cook egg well.

**Talk to your doctor before you start** if your baby has severe eczema, an existing
food allergy, or a strong family history. Some infants need allergen introduction
supervised.

## Purées or baby-led weaning

Both work. The evidence does not clearly favour either for healthy term infants, and
most families end up mixing the two, spoon-feeding some things and handing over
others.

Choose what you can sustain. A parent who finds baby-led weaning stressful will do
it badly; a parent who finds purée-making tedious will stop.

## Milk is still the main event

Through the first year, breastmilk or formula remains the primary source of
nutrition. In the early months your baby uses solids to practise: managing
texture, coordinating a swallow, working out that food exists at all.

If solids start crowding out milk feeds before nine months, or your baby loses
interest in milk, raise it at your next check-up.
