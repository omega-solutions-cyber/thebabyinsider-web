If you have opened any parenting app in the last three years, you have met the
wake window chart: a tidy grid promising that a six-week-old should be awake for
exactly 60 minutes, and that missing the window is why bedtime went badly.

The charts are useful. They are also, almost without exception, uncited.

## Where the numbers come from

Wake windows are not a clinical measure. They are a practical heuristic that
emerged from sleep consultants and parent communities, loosely anchored to real
observations about how infant sleep pressure builds in the first year.

That does not make them worthless — sleep pressure is real, and overtired babies
genuinely do settle less easily. It does mean the specific numbers deserve less
authority than the confident formatting implies.

## What the research supports

Three things are reasonably well established, and the
[American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/default.aspx)
is the reference point for all of them:

- Total sleep need in the newborn period is high and highly variable — commonly
  cited as 14 to 17 hours across 24 hours, with a wide normal range on either
  side.
- Newborn sleep is distributed in short bouts around the clock. Consolidated
  night sleep is a developmental milestone, not a training outcome.
- Infants show observable behavioural cues before they become overtired, and
  those cues are individual.

Notably absent from that list: any evidence that a specific number of minutes is
correct for a specific week of life.

## Watch the baby, not the clock

The practical version is unglamorous. Early tired cues — a fixed stare,
disengagement, jerky movements, the first yawn — are a better signal than a
timer, because they come from your baby rather than from an average.

Use a chart as a rough expectation of when to _start looking_ for cues. Use your
baby to decide what to do.

## When to raise it with a clinician

Variation is normal. These are not:

- A newborn who is persistently difficult to wake for feeds
- Poor weight gain or fewer wet nappies than expected
- Breathing that seems laboured during sleep
- A sudden, sustained change in sleep with no obvious cause

Any of those is a conversation with your own paediatrician, not a scheduling
problem.
