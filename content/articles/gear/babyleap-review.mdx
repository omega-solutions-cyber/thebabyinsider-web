## The drawbacks, first

**The trial is short.** Seven days is not long enough to evaluate an app whose
value is a _weekly_ plan. You get one cycle.

**The best feature is paywalled.** The AI assistant — which handles the 2am
"is this normal" questions better than searching does — is premium-only. That
is a defensible commercial decision and an irritating user experience.

**Monthly pricing is bad value.** The annual plan is reasonable for what it is.
The monthly plan is priced to push you to annual, and it works.

**It is another app asking for your baby's data.** The privacy policy is
readable and the data is not sold, but you should read it yourself rather than
take our word for it, given who is writing this.

## What it does well

**Milestones as ranges.** This is the design decision we rate most highly, and
it is the one most competitors get wrong. Development is shown as "most babies
do this between X and Y", not as a date your baby can miss. Given how much
anxiety these apps generate, that framing matters more than any feature.

**The weekly plan is specific.** Activities are concrete and tied to what your
baby is actually working on, rather than generic "talk to your baby"
encouragement. This is the feature that justifies the subscription if anything
does.

**One app instead of two.** Feeds, sleep and nappies log alongside development,
so you are not reconciling two apps at 3am.

**Clinical review.** Content is reviewed by clinicians before publication — the
same standard we hold our own articles to.

## Who it is not for

If your problem is specifically **sleep**, a dedicated sleep app will serve you
better; BabyLeap's sleep logging is competent rather than exceptional.

If you dislike subscriptions on principle, a one-off purchase app will annoy you
less, even if it does less.
