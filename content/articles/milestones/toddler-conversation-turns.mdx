Between their first and third birthdays, your child's brain is wiring itself faster
than it ever will again. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child puts it at more than
a million new neural connections every second in the early years.

Which connections stay and which get pruned depends heavily on what happens around
them. So researchers set out to answer a simple question: of everything parents do with
toddlers, what actually shows up years later?

They put small recorders on 146 children, some as young as two months, and captured
entire days of what they heard. Then they waited. A decade later, they found the same
children, now aged 9 to 14, and tested their IQ, vocabulary and verbal comprehension.

One measure from the toddler recordings stood out. Taken between **18 and 24 months**,
it accounted for **14 to 27 percent** of the differences in those scores ten years
later, after the researchers had already controlled for family income and education.

It wasn't how many books were in the house. It wasn't how many words the children heard.

It was how many times a day someone **talked _with_ them**.

## "My kid hears me talk all day." So did theirs.

That was our first reaction too, so it's worth being precise about what was counted.

The recorders didn't just measure talking. They counted **conversational turns**: your
toddler makes a sound or points, you respond within a few seconds, they respond back.
Narrating your day out loud while they play is lovely. It also counts as zero turns.

A few years later, a team at MIT and Harvard put children in a brain scanner to see
whether this showed up physically. It did. The more conversational turns a child had at
home, the more their **Broca's area**, the brain region most tied to speech and
language, lit up while they listened to a story.

The number of words those children heard? It barely mattered. **Turns predicted brain
activity. Raw word count didn't.** The effect held across rich and poor households
alike.

<KeyTakeaways>

- In a 10-year study, back-and-forth exchanges at 18–24 months were the strongest predictor of language and IQ scores in late childhood.
- Brain scans linked those exchanges, not the number of words heard, to activity in the brain's main language region.
- It costs nothing, and you already do it sometimes. Most parents just don't do it on purpose.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Why a screen can't do it for you

If this sounds like another "play Mozart to your baby" claim, here is the experiment
that convinced us it isn't.

Researchers at the University of Washington took 9-month-old American babies and gave
them twelve short sessions with a Mandarin speaker. One group got a real person,
playing and talking with them. A second group watched the same speaker, saying the same
things, on video. A third heard only the audio.

Afterwards, the babies who'd had a live person could tell apart Mandarin speech sounds
about as well as babies raised in Taiwan.

The video and audio groups learned **nothing**. Same words, same voice, same amount of
time. The only missing ingredient was someone responding to them.

That's the part most "brain-boosting" products miss. Toddler brains aren't recording
devices. They learn through the loop: I do something, you react, I adjust.

## The technique has a name

Developmental scientists call it **serve and return**. Your child serves (a babble, a
point, a word, a look) and you return it: name the thing, ask about it, add one word to
what they said. Then you wait for their next serve.

It sounds almost too simple to matter. The catch is that what counts as a serve, and
what a good return looks like, changes fast between 12 and 24 months:

- **Around 12 months**, the serve is often a point or a sound. The return is naming the thing: "Dog! That's a big dog."
- **Around 18 months**, it's single words. The return adds one: "Ball." "Red ball!"
- **Around 24 months**, two-word phrases. The return expands and asks: "Daddy go." "Daddy's going to work. Where's Daddy going?"

Return a serve at the wrong level and the loop stalls. Too simple and they lose interest;
too complex and they go quiet. This is why the same advice ("just talk to them more")
works brilliantly for one family and does nothing for another.

## What the research doesn't say

Because it matters: these studies show a strong, consistent link, not a guarantee. No
single habit adds IQ points on demand, and anyone promising a specific number is selling
something. Children develop across wide, normal ranges, and a quiet toddler is usually
just a quiet toddler.

But of all the things competing for your attention in these two years, this one is
free, takes no equipment, fits into nappy changes and car rides, and has a decade of
data behind it. If your child isn't using any words by around 18 months, or loses words
they had, raise it with your paediatrician rather than waiting.
