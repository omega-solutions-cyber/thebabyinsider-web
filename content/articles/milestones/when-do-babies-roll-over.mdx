Ask the internet when babies roll over and you will get a number: four months.
Ask a paediatrician and you will get a range, a question about tummy time, and a
gentle reminder that the date matters much less than the trajectory.

The range is the accurate answer.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Tummy to back** typically appears between three and five months.
- **Back to tummy** typically follows between four and seven months.
- Rolling is the first milestone with a **safety consequence**: once your baby shows signs of rolling, swaddling with the arms in must stop.
- Babies who spend less awake time on their tummies often roll later. This is usually about opportunity, not ability.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Why tummy-to-back comes first

Rolling from the tummy onto the back is mechanically easier. A baby pushing up
on their forearms during tummy time only has to shift their weight slightly too
far to one side and gravity finishes the job — which is why the first roll is
so often an accident that startles everyone involved, including the baby.

Going the other way is a genuine strength milestone. Back to tummy needs core
rotation, head control and enough shoulder strength to clear the arm that gets
trapped underneath. That is why it usually arrives one to two months later.

## The safety change nobody mentions

This is the part that matters more than the date.

Once a baby shows _any_ sign of rolling — including rocking onto their side —
swaddling with the arms contained should stop. A swaddled baby who ends up face
down cannot push up or turn their head effectively.

Safe sleep guidance otherwise stays the same: back to sleep for every sleep, on
a firm flat surface, with nothing else in the cot. If your baby rolls themselves
onto their tummy during sleep once they can roll both ways independently, you do
not need to keep flipping them back — but you should still place them on their
back to start.

## Tummy time is the lever you actually control

Rolling is a strength skill, and strength comes from practice. Babies who get
more awake time on their tummies tend to roll earlier — not because they are
developmentally ahead, but because they have had more repetitions.

Practical version:

- Start from the first weeks, in very short bursts, on your chest if the floor
  is unpopular
- Aim to build toward roughly an hour a day in total by around three months,
  broken into many short sessions
- Get down at eye level — most tummy-time protest is boredom rather than effort
- Count time on your chest, across your lap, and in supported side-lying

## When to raise it with your paediatrician

Wide variation is the norm. These specific things are worth a conversation:

- No rolling in either direction by around six months
- Rolling that only ever happens to one side, or a persistent head turn to one side
- **Loss of a skill your baby previously had** — this always warrants a same-week call
- Stiffness, floppiness, or arms and legs that consistently move very differently
  from one another
- No weight-bearing on the forearms during tummy time by four months

None of these mean something is wrong. All of them are better assessed by
someone who can examine your baby than by a chart.
