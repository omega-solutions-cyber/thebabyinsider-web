Ask the internet when babies roll over and you get a number: four months. Ask a
paediatrician and you get a range, a question about how much tummy time your baby
gets, and a reminder to watch the trajectory rather than the calendar.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Tummy to back** typically appears between three and five months.
- **Back to tummy** typically follows between four and seven months.
- Rolling is the first milestone with a **safety consequence**: once your baby shows signs of rolling, stop swaddling with the arms in.
- Babies who spend less awake time on their tummies often roll later. They have had fewer chances to practise.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Tummy to back comes first

Rolling from the tummy onto the back is mechanically easier. A baby pushing up on
their forearms only has to shift their weight a little too far to one side, and
gravity finishes the job. A first roll usually arrives as an accident and startles
everyone present, your baby included.

Going the other way takes strength. Back to tummy needs core rotation, head control
and enough shoulder power to clear the arm that gets trapped underneath, so it
usually shows up one to two months later.

## Stop swaddling once they roll

Once your baby shows _any_ sign of rolling, including rocking onto their side, stop
swaddling with the arms contained. A swaddled baby who ends up face down cannot push
up or turn their head.

Safe sleep guidance otherwise holds: back to sleep for every sleep, on a firm flat
surface, with nothing else in the cot. Once your baby can roll both ways
independently and rolls onto their tummy in the night, you can leave them there.
Keep putting them down on their back.

## Tummy time is the part you control

Strength comes from repetition, so babies who get more awake time on their tummies
tend to roll earlier.

- Start in the first weeks, in very short bursts, on your chest if the floor is
  unpopular
- Build toward roughly an hour a day in total by around three months, broken into
  many short sessions
- Get down at eye level; most tummy-time protest is boredom rather than effort
- Count time on your chest, across your lap, and in supported side-lying

## When to raise it with your paediatrician

Babies vary widely. Raise these:

- No rolling in either direction by around six months
- Rolling that only ever happens to one side, or a persistent head turn to one side
- **Loss of a skill your baby previously had.** This always warrants a same-week call
- Stiffness, floppiness, or arms and legs that consistently move very differently
  from one another
- No weight-bearing on the forearms during tummy time by four months

None of these means something is wrong. Someone who can examine your baby will read
them better than a chart will.
