There is a particular kind of tiredness that sleep does not fix.

It is the point where you can get through the day competently — feeds done,
nappies changed, nobody harmed — while feeling almost nothing about it. Where
your baby cries and your first reaction is not concern but a flat, exhausted
dread. Where you are fairly sure everyone else is finding this easier.

That is not a personality defect. It has a name and a described shape.

<KeyTakeaways>
  - Parental burnout has three documented features: **exhaustion**, **emotional distancing** from
  your child, and **a sense of ineffectiveness** as a parent. - It is distinct from postnatal
  depression, though the two overlap and often co-occur. - It affects partners and non-birthing
  parents too, and is under-recognised in them. - It responds to **redistributed load and real
  recovery time** — not to trying harder.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What it looks like

**Exhaustion that rest does not touch.** Emotional and cognitive, not just
physical. Some of the sharpest presentations are in parents whose babies sleep
reasonably well.

**Emotional distancing.** Going through the motions. Doing the tasks without
feeling connected to the person you are doing them for. This is the symptom
parents are most ashamed of and least likely to mention, which is unfortunate,
because it is the most characteristic one.

**A sense of ineffectiveness.** Comparing yourself unfavourably with the parent
you meant to be, and concluding you are failing at something everyone else
manages.

## How it differs from postnatal depression

Burnout is **role-specific**. It is about parenting. A parent with burnout can
often still enjoy work, friends or a run — they simply have nothing left for the
one domain.

Depression is not confined that way. It flattens mood across everything,
including things unconnected to the baby, and often brings sleep and appetite
changes, hopelessness, and sometimes thoughts of self-harm.

They coexist frequently, and the practical distinction matters less than the
response: both warrant a professional conversation.

<Callout type="warning">
  If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, contact your doctor, midwife or emergency
  services now. These thoughts are more common than most parents realise, they are treatable, and
  disclosing them is not a step toward having your child removed.
</Callout>

## What actually helps

**Redistribute load rather than adding tasks.** Most "self-care" advice adds an
item to the list of things you are failing to do. The useful question is what
comes off the list, and who else can hold it.

**Recovery has to be reliable, not heroic.** A single spa day does nothing. Two
predictable hours a week that you can count on does considerably more, because
anticipation is part of the mechanism.

**Say it out loud to one person.** The distancing symptom thrives on secrecy.
Naming it to a partner, a friend or a health visitor breaks most of its force.

**Lower the standard deliberately.** The first year is a period to be survived
competently, not optimised. Reducing what counts as a good day is a legitimate
intervention.

**Get professional help if it persists.** Talking therapies work for this. Your
GP, health visitor or paediatrician can point you at what is available locally,
and this is a completely ordinary thing to raise.

## For partners and non-birthing parents

Screening is usually aimed at birthing parents, so burnout in partners routinely
goes unnoticed — including by the partner, who often assumes they have no
standing to be struggling because they did not give birth.

They do. The same three features apply, and the same responses work.

## The sleep connection

Chronic sleep deprivation is a direct contributor, which is why burnout so often
peaks alongside the

Improving sleep helps. It is rarely the whole answer, and "fix the baby's sleep" is not a substitute
for support.
